# 3.0. Data Quality and Issues

## 3.0.1. The Concept of Data Quality

Data quality is the foundation of any analytical or modeling endeavor. The principle of "garbage in, garbage out" dictates that the quality of your outputs is strictly bounded by the quality of your inputs. 

In statistical modelling, data quality refers to the degree to which a dataset accurately represents the true state of the phenomenon being measured. High-quality data is accurate, complete, consistent, and reliable. Low-quality data introduces errors that propagate through every subsequent step of the analysis, leading to misleading conclusions and poor model performance.

To emphasize the foundational rule of data quality, we state it formally:

$$
\text{Output Quality} \leq \text{Input Quality}
$$

## 3.0.2. Noise and Measurement Errors

Transitioning from the general concept of data quality to specific issues, we first examine noise. Noise refers to the random variance or error in measured values. It is the distortion of the true signal caused by imperfect measurement instruments, environmental interference, or human error.

### 3.0.2.1. The Mathematical Nature of Noise

Mathematically, an observed value $$x$$ can be decomposed into the true signal $$s$$ and the noise $$\epsilon$$:

$$
x = s + \epsilon
$$

where:
- $$x$$ = observed value
- $$s$$ = true underlying signal
- $$\epsilon$$ = random noise component

To emphasize the decomposition of an observed value, we restate the formula:

$$
x = s + \epsilon
$$

Noise typically has a mean of zero, meaning it does not systematically bias the results, but it increases the variance of the data. This increased variance reduces the statistical power of hypothesis tests and makes it harder for machine learning models to learn the true underlying patterns.

>[!Note]
> Noise is random and unsystematic. While it obscures the true signal, it does not pull the average estimate in a specific direction.

## 3.0.3. Outliers and Anomalies

While noise is random and pervasive, outliers are specific, extreme observations that deviate markedly from the rest of the dataset. 

### 3.0.3.1. Distinguishing Outliers from Noise

An outlier is a data point that differs significantly from other observations. It may arise from:
- Measurement error (a malfunctioning sensor)
- Data entry error (typing 1000 instead of 100)
- Genuine rare events (a billionaire in an income dataset)

The critical distinction is that outliers are individual data points, whereas noise affects the entire dataset to varying degrees. 

>[!Warning]
> Deleting an outlier without investigation is intellectual laziness. An outlier might be a data entry error that must be corrected, or it might be the most important insight in the dataset. You must diagnose the cause before taking action.

### 3.0.3.2. Impact of Outliers on Statistical Measures

Outliers have a disproportionate impact on statistical measures that rely on squaring differences, such as the mean and the standard deviation. 

The sample mean is calculated as:

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}
$$

Because every $$x_i$$ contributes directly to the sum, a single extreme value can pull the mean far away from the center of the bulk of the data. Conversely, the median is robust to outliers because it depends only on the rank order of the values, not their magnitude.

## 3.0.4. Missing Values

Transitioning from extreme values to absent values, we encounter missing data. Missing values occur when no value is stored for an attribute in a particular record. This is one of the most common and challenging data quality issues in real-world datasets.

### 3.0.4.1. Mechanisms of Missingness

Understanding why data is missing is crucial for selecting the correct handling technique. Statisticians classify missing data into three mechanisms.

**Missing Completely at Random (MCAR)**: The probability of a value being missing is entirely unrelated to any observed or unobserved data. The missingness is purely random.

**Missing at Random (MAR)**: The probability of a value being missing is related to the observed data, but not to the missing value itself. For example, younger users might be less likely to report their income, but the missingness depends only on age (which is observed), not on the income itself.

**Missing Not at Random (MNAR)**: The probability of a value being missing is related to the unobserved missing value itself. For example, people with very high incomes might be more likely to refuse to report their income. 

>[!Warning]
> MNAR is the most dangerous mechanism. If the missingness depends on the unobserved value, simply imputing the mean will introduce severe bias into your dataset.

## 3.0.5. Duplicate Data and Inconsistencies

Another critical threat to data integrity is the presence of duplicate records and logical inconsistencies.

### 3.0.5.1. Duplicate Records

Duplicates occur when the same entity is represented by multiple rows in the dataset. This often happens during data integration when merging multiple sources, or when system errors cause a transaction to be logged twice.

If left unaddressed, duplicates artificially inflate the sample size $$n$$ and give undue weight to the duplicated records. The sample mean formula:

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}
$$

will be skewed toward the values of the duplicated records, violating the assumption of independent observations.

### 3.0.5.2. Logical Inconsistencies

Inconsistencies occur when data violates defined business rules or logical constraints. Examples include:
- A start date that occurs after an end date.
- A negative value for an attribute that must be positive, such as age or height.
- A total sum that does not equal the sum of its parts.

Inconsistencies indicate fundamental flaws in the data collection or entry process and must be resolved before any analysis can proceed.

## 3.0.6. Example of Diagnosing Data Quality Issues

To solidify the concepts of data quality, we apply diagnostic reasoning to a concrete scenario.

Suppose:
- A dataset of customer ages: 25, 28, 22, -5, 30, 29, 27, 200, 26, 24
- We need to identify and classify the data quality issues.

### Step 1: Scan for Logical Inconsistencies
Reviewing the values, we observe an age of -5. Age cannot be negative. This is a logical inconsistency and a clear data entry error.

### Step 2: Scan for Outliers
We observe an age of 200. While theoretically possible in a biological sense, in a standard customer dataset, this is an extreme deviation. This is an outlier, likely a data entry error (e.g., typing 200 instead of 20).

### Step 3: Assess the Impact on the Mean
If we calculate the mean including the errors:
$$
\bar{x} = \frac{25 + 28 + 22 - 5 + 30 + 29 + 27 + 200 + 26 + 24}{10} = \frac{406}{10} = 40.6
$$

### Step 4: Assess the Impact After Correction
If we remove or correct the invalid values (-5 and 200), the sum of the remaining 8 valid values is 211.
$$
\bar{x}_{\text{corrected}} = \frac{211}{8} = 26.375
$$

### Step 5: Final Diagnosis
The original mean of **40.6** is highly distorted by the outlier and inconsistency. The corrected mean of **26.375** accurately reflects the central tendency of the valid data. This demonstrates how a single poor-quality record can fundamentally alter statistical conclusions.

## 3.0.7. Conclusions

Data quality is not a secondary concern; it is the prerequisite for valid statistical inference. Noise obscures patterns, outliers distort measures of central tendency, missing values reduce statistical power and introduce bias, and duplicates violate the assumption of independence.

### 3.0.7.1. Summary of Data Quality Issues

The following table summarizes the primary data quality issues, their characteristics, and their primary impact on analysis.

| Issue | Nature | Primary Impact |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Noise | Random variance | Increases variance, reduces statistical power |
| Outliers | Extreme individual values | Distorts mean and standard deviation |
| Missing Values | Absent data points | Reduces sample size, introduces bias if MNAR |
| Duplicates | Repeated records | Violates independence, artificially inflates $$n$$ |

>[!Tip]
> Always profile your data before modeling. A few minutes spent identifying noise, outliers, and missing values will save hours of debugging misleading model results later.
