# 8.1.3. Measures of Spread and Variability

Central tendency alone is fundamentally incomplete. Two datasets can possess the exact same mean but exhibit vastly different spreads. 

>[!Tip]
>Measures of variability quantify how dispersed the data points are around the center.

The **range** is the simplest measure of spread, calculated as the difference between the maximum and minimum values:

$$\text{Range} = \max(x) - \min(x)$$

While easy to compute, the range is highly sensitive to outliers because it relies entirely on the two extreme values.

- To capture the dispersion of all data points, we use **variance** and **standard deviation**. <br>
- These measures calculate the average deviation from the mean.

The population standard deviation is defined as:

$$
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \mu)^2}{n}}
$$

where:
- $$\sigma$$ = population standard deviation
- $$x_i$$ = individual data points
- $$\mu$$ = population mean
- $$n$$ = number of data points

The squaring operation in the numerator ensures that deviations above and below the mean do not cancel each other out, and it heavily penalizes large deviations.

To emphasize the standard deviation formula, we restate it:

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \mu)^2}{n}}$$

### 3.1 Quartiles and Data Dispersion

Quartiles divide the sorted data into four equal parts, providing a more robust view of dispersion than the range.

| Quartile | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $$Q_1$$ | 25th percentile (lower quartile) |
| $$Q_2$$ | 50th percentile (the median) |
| $$Q_3$$ | 75th percentile (upper quartile) |

- The difference between the third and first quartiles is the Interquartile Range ($$\text{IQR}$$),
- which measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data and is highly resistant to outliers.
