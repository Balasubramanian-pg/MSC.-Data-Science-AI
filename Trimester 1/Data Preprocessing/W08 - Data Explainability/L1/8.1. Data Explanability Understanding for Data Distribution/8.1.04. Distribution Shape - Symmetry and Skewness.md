# 8.1.4. Distribution Shape: Symmetry and Skewness

<img width="639" height="500" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/ef4c00c9-a339-46b3-8860-b177e345511e" />

Beyond center and spread, the overall shape of the distribution reveals critical information about the underlying data generating process.

A **symmetric distribution** has a balanced shape on both sides of the center. In a perfectly symmetric distribution, the mean, median, and mode are all equal. There is no skewness.

>[!Note]
>**Skewness** quantifies the asymmetry of the data distribution. It indicates whether the data tends to trail off more on one side than the other.

<img width="2048" height="1152" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/f0c0dfac-0439-42d3-9d3c-db404c2dca9c" />

### 4.1 Positive Skewness

In a positively skewed distribution, the tail extends to the right. 

The relationship between the measures of central tendency is typically:

$$
\text{Mean} > \text{Median} > \text{Mode}
$$

The classic example of positive skewness is household income. The majority of the population earns a moderate or low income, clustering the mode and median on the left. However, a small number of billionaires earn extremely high incomes, creating a long right tail that drags the mean upward.

### 4.2 Negative Skewness

In a negatively skewed distribution, the tail extends to the left.

The relationship between the measures of central tendency is typically:

$$
\text{Mean} < \text{Median} < \text{Mode}
$$

An example of negative skewness is human lifespan in developed countries. The majority of the population lives to a relatively old age, clustering the bulk of the data on the right. A small left tail represents the unfortunate cases of early mortality, pulling the mean slightly below the median.

>[!Note]
> Different central tendency measures react differently to distribution shape. Recognizing this hidden statistical insight is foundational for robust statistics, econometrics, and machine learning preprocessing.
