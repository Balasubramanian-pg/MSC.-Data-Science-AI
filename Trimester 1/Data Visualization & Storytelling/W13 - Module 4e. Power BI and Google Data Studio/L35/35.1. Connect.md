# 35.2. Visual Dashboard Design in Business Intelligence

## 35.2.1. From Aggregate Metrics to Decision Insights

Raw transactional data represents the ultimate source of truth, but its sheer volume makes it incomprehensible to decision-makers. Visual dashboards solve this problem by converting hundreds of thousands of individual rows into structured visual components. 

In any typical sales database, a point estimate like the total sales must be represented dynamically to show high-level trends alongside granular variations. These visual summaries allow analysts to immediately detect geographic variations, product-specific deficits, and categorical performance trends.

## 35.2.2. Why Static Reports Are Fundamentally Incomplete

A static report provides only a single slice of data frozen in time, failing to capture the dynamic relationships between different business dimensions. When an analyst presents a single static chart showing national sales, they obscure the localized regional variances and categorical performance drivers.

This limitation is resolved by introducing interactive business intelligence dashboards, where elements respond dynamically to user input.

The key insight of modern visualization is:

>[!Note]
> A dashboard is an interactive, multi-dimensional query system disguised as a visual report.

When a user interacts with one visual element, such as clicking a specific state on a map, all other visuals on the page automatically filter to display data for that state only. This real-time cross-filtering provides a localized view of performance without requiring multiple *ad hoc* static reports.

## 35.2.3. Mathematical Formulation of Calculated Fields

When designing interactive dashboards, we often need to present complex ratios that are not stored in the underlying data source. For instance, calculating a state's overall profitability requires a calculated field to evaluate the cumulative performance of all transactions within that state.

A common error is taking the average of individual transaction margins, which fails to account for varying transaction volumes. Instead, we must define the weighted aggregate profit percentage.

The formula for the calculated field **Profit Percentage** is:

$$
\text{Profit Percentage} = \frac{\sum \text{Profit}}{\sum \text{Sales}}
$$

where:

- $$ \text{Profit Percentage} $$ = the overall weighted profitability ratio

- $$ \sum \text{Profit} $$ = the sum of profits across all filtered transactions

- $$ \sum \text{Sales} $$ = the sum of sales across all filtered transactions

This formula represents a ratio of sums rather than a sum of ratios. By using:

$$
\text{Profit Percentage} = \frac{\sum \text{Profit}}{\sum \text{Sales}}
$$

the dashboard ensures that large-scale, high-value transactions correctly weight the final percentage, maintaining mathematical accuracy regardless of how the user filters or drills down into the data.

## 35.2.4. Step-by-Step Example of Calculated Field Evaluation

To understand why this ratio-of-sums calculation is critical, consider a localized dataset for a single state.

Suppose:
- Transaction 1 Profit is:

$$
200
$$

- Transaction 1 Sales is:

$$
1000
$$

- Transaction 2 Profit is:

$$
-50
$$

- Transaction 2 Sales is:

$$
500
$$

- Transaction 3 Profit is:

$$
150
$$

- Transaction 3 Sales is:

$$
500
$$

### Step 1: Sum the Total Sales
Add all individual sales values to find the denominator:

$$
\sum \text{Sales} = 1000 + 500 + 500 = 2000
$$

### Step 2: Sum the Total Profit
Add all individual profit values (including losses) to find the numerator:

$$
\sum \text{Profit} = 200 + (-50) + 150 = 300
$$

### Step 3: Set Up the Calculated Field Formula
State the ratio-of-sums equation:

$$
\text{Profit Percentage} = \frac{\sum \text{Profit}}{\sum \text{Sales}}
$$

### Step 4: Divide Aggregate Profit by Aggregate Sales
Substitute the values into the formula to find the decimal ratio:

$$
\text{Profit Percentage} = \frac{300}{2000} = 0.15
$$

### Step 5: Convert and Express as Percentage
Multiply the decimal ratio by 100 to get the final weighted percentage:

$$
0.15 \times 100 = 15\%
$$

The final weighted profit percentage for the state is **15%**.

## 35.2.5. Doughnut Charts for Categorical Share

To display how different categories or regions contribute to the total sales volume, we employ part-to-whole visualizations like doughnut charts. A doughnut chart functions similarly to a standard pie chart but features a hollow center, which improves readability and leaves room for displaying central callouts such as total metrics.

The following table shows the distribution of sales and metrics across different geographic regions as displayed in a regional sales doughnut chart.

| Region | Sales Share | Performance Level |
|----------|:----------:|----------:|
| West | 35% | High |
| East | 30% | High |
| Central | 20% | Moderate |
| South | 15% | Low |

By using a doughnut chart, the user can easily observe that the West and East regions together make up more than half of the total sales. In the dashboard, we can configure these charts to show the percentage of sales rather than the raw record count, ensuring that the metric aligns with the core business goals.

## 35.2.6. Geographical Mapping for Spatial Analysis

While regional share provides a high-level view, businesses need to analyze local variations to make distribution and pricing decisions. A geographical choropleth map provides spatial context by highlighting individual administrative boundaries, such as states, based on a specific performance metric.

In our dashboard, the map displays the calculated state-by-state profit percentage:

$$
\text{Profit Percentage} = \frac{\sum \text{Profit}}{\sum \text{Sales}}
$$

By using a green-to-red or light-to-dark color gradient, the map immediately signals which states are generating high margins and which states are operating at a loss. If a user selects a specific state on this map, the surrounding charts instantly filter to that state's data, allowing analysts to investigate localized operational issues.

## 35.2.7. Tree Maps with Drill-Down Functionality

Geographical trends must be analyzed in tandem with product performance, which requires a visualization capable of handling deeply nested categorical hierarchies. A tree map displays hierarchical data as a set of nested rectangles, where the area of each rectangle represents its metric size (such as sales volume) and the color can represent another metric (such as profit margin).

In our sales dashboard, the tree map is structured with two levels of dimensions:
- **Category** (e.g., Technology, Furniture, Office Supplies)
- **Subcategory** (e.g., Phones, Chairs, Paper)

To navigate this complex structure without cluttering the screen, we enable the **drill-down** feature. When drill-down is enabled:
- Users see the top-level categories first.
- Clicking on a category like Furniture drills down to show only its subcategories (Chairs, Tables, Bookcases).

This hierarchy allows the dashboard to maintain a clean layout while preserving deep analytical pathways.

## 35.2.8. Waterfall Charts for Cumulative Contribution

While a tree map excels at showing total volume, a waterfall chart is ideal for showing how individual elements sequentially add to or subtract from a cumulative total. This is particularly useful for tracking how different product segments or subcategories contribute to the overall corporate profit.

In a horizontal waterfall chart:
- The vertical axis lists the categories or subcategories in descending order of their positive contribution.
- The horizontal axis measures the cumulative profit:

$$
\text{Profit} = \sum \text{Profit}
$$

- Positive contributions are colored green, negative contributions (representing losses) are colored red, and the final bar represents the total aggregated profit.

For example, if the Phone subcategory contributes highly to profit, its green bar extends the cumulative total. Conversely, if Tables operate at a net loss, its red bar shifts the cumulative total backward. This visualization allows executives to immediately identify which product lines are driving profits and which are eroding the bottom line.

## 35.2.9. Principles of Effective Dashboard Design

Creating a successful dashboard requires balancing data density with visual clarity. The layout must guide the user's eye naturally from high-level summaries to detailed breakdowns.

### 9.1 Layout and Spacing
Dashboards operate under strict space constraints. Placing too many elements on a single page causes cognitive overload and dilutes the signal-to-noise ratio. To maximize readability, organize the elements hierarchically:
- Place high-level key performance indicators (KPIs) at the top.
- Place aggregate share charts (doughnut charts) in the upper-middle section.
- Place spatial maps and detailed waterfall charts in the lower, larger sections of the page.

Ensure every visual has a defined boundary, and use border shadows to physically separate different analytical spaces on the screen.

### 9.2 Color Schemes and Themes
Color should always be used to convey specific statistical meaning, not for arbitrary decoration.
- Use a single cohesive color theme to maintain a professional appearance.
- Use contrasting gradients (such as light-to-dark blue) to show progression in values.
- Use divergent color scales (like red-to-green) only when a clear mid-point, such as a zero-profit line, exists.

Ensure that the chosen color palette is accessible to color-blind individuals while still maintaining logical business associations (such as green representing profit and red representing loss).

### 9.3 Interactivity and Cross-Filtering
The defining feature of a modern dashboard is real-time interactive exploration. By enabling cross-filtering, every chart acts as a filter control for the others.
- Selecting a segment in a waterfall chart filters the geographical map to show where those segment sales occurred.
- Selecting a state on the map updates the tree map to show the local product mix.

This bi-directional interactivity allows users to conduct self-guided root-cause analysis without requiring technical database querying skills.

## 35.2.10. Common Dashboarding Mistakes to Avoid

Even with advanced tools like Power BI and Google Looker Studio, visual designers frequently introduce errors that mislead decision-makers.

### 10.1 Key Warnings

>[!Warning]
> **Unweighted Averaging:** Never calculate the average of transaction ratios (such as profit margin percentages) directly. Always calculate the weighted aggregate using total sums:
> $$ \text{Profit Percentage} = \frac{\sum \text{Profit}}{\sum \text{Sales}} $$
> Failing to do so can severely distort performance metrics for regions with high-volume, low-margin sales.

>[!Warning]
> **Visual Clutter and Lack of Borders:** Do not crowd too many widgets into a single workspace. Without adequate spacing and border shadows, separate visualizations merge into a confusing mass of color, making it difficult for the eye to distinguish between different data stories.

>[!Warning]
> **Misaligned Color Context:** Avoid using highly saturated dark colors across all charts simultaneously, as it fatigues the user. Additionally, never use red for positive metrics or green for negative metrics, as this directly violates universal visual conventions and causes immediate user confusion.

## 35.2.11. Sharing and Deployment Options

Once a dashboard is fully constructed and styled, it must be deployed to the target audience. Business intelligence tools offer multiple distribution pathways:
- **PDF Export:** Allows users to download a static, offline snapshot of the current dashboard state, ideal for periodic executive summaries or physical printouts.
- **Interactive Cloud Sharing:** Provides users with a direct web link or access permissions to interact with the live dashboard in their browser without requiring local software or licenses.
- **Native Database Connectors:** Dashboards can be linked directly to production databases (such as SQL Server). This connection enables scheduled daily refreshes, ensuring that the dashboard automatically displays up-to-date transaction records without manual intervention.

## 35.2.12. Conclusions

A well-designed dashboard transforms a massive transactional database into an intuitive, interactive analytical workspace. By utilizing the correct visual components, maintaining strict layout principles, and using proper weighted calculations, analysts can present data clearly.

The following table summarizes and compares the key visualization types used to build our sales and profit dashboard.

| Visualization | Primary Dimension | Primary Metric | Core Business Purpose |
|----------|:----------:|:----------:|----------:|
| **Doughnut Chart** | Category or Region | Sales Volume | Shows categorical and regional share |
| **Geographical Map** | State or Country | Profit Percentage | Identifies spatial profitability trends |
| **Tree Map** | Category & Subcategory | Sales Volume | Displays nested structures with drill-down |
| **Waterfall Chart** | Segment or Subcategory | Cumulative Profit | Tracks cumulative profit contributions |

By combining these visual elements into a single cohesive layout, organizations transition from static, descriptive reporting to active, exploratory data analysis.
