# 9.3. Conditions for Factor Analysis

## 9.3.1. Foundational Assumptions: Ensuring Factorability

Factor Analysis is not a universal black box capable of organizing any dataset. 

Because the entire methodology strictly relies on analyzing the patterns of correlation between variables to uncover hidden latent constructs, the raw data must possess reliable underlying associations. If the dataset does not contain these embedded structures, the resulting mathematical output will be structurally unstable and interpretatively useless. 

Therefore, analysts must rigorously validate both the architectural data characteristics and the statistical properties before proceeding to factor extraction.

## 9.3.2. Data Characteristics: The Quality Control Phase

Before executing the model, the dataset must satisfy three fundamental architectural prerequisites.

### 2.1 Level of Measurement
Factor Analysis strictly calculates correlation matrices, typically using Pearson’s:
$$ r $$
Therefore, the input data must be continuous (interval or ratio level). While quasi-continuous ordinal data, such as 5-point Likert scales, are frequently used in market research, applying standard factor analysis to binary or heavily constrained categorical data yields severely biased results.

### 2.2 Sample Size Adequacy
The algorithm relies entirely on the stability of the computed factor loadings. A small sample size inevitably leads to overfitting, where the model identifies random noise specific to that particular sample rather than generalizable population structures. 

The standard minimum threshold is the 10:1 ratio: an analyst must have at least ten distinct observations for every single variable included in the matrix. 

### 2.3 The Assumption of Linearity
Factor Analysis assumes that as one variable changes, the associated variables change at a consistent linear rate. If two variables share a strong U-shaped or exponential relationship, the Pearson correlation will mathematically underestimate their true association. Because the algorithm relies on these correlation coefficients to build the factors, it will completely ignore non-linear structures.

## 9.3.3. Statistical Properties: Diagnostic Tests for Factorability

Once the data architecture is validated, analysts must mathematically prove that the variables are sufficiently correlated to form latent factors. This is the factorability test.

### 3.1 Visual Matrix Inspection
The correlation matrix is the DNA of the analysis. Analysts must visually identify pockets or clusters of correlation. 
- High inter-correlations, mathematically defined as:
  $$ |r| > 0.3 $$
  suggest that the variables likely share a latent source. 
- Conversely, extreme correlations defined as:
  $$ |r| > 0.9 $$
  indicate dangerous multicollinearity, meaning the variables are redundant and will destabilize the matrix inversion process.

### 3.2 Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity
This is the formal baseline statistical gatekeeper. The test evaluates whether the correlation matrix deviates significantly from an Identity Matrix.

An Identity Matrix contains 1s on the diagonal and absolute 0s everywhere else, indicating total independence.
The hypothesis structure is:
- **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** The correlation matrix is an exact identity matrix.
- **Objective:** The analyst requires a significant result:
  $$ p < 0.05 $$
  to reject the null hypothesis, formally proving that the variables share sufficient variance to proceed.

### 3.3 The KMO Measure of Sampling Adequacy
While Bartlett’s Test confirms that correlations exist, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure evaluates whether those correlations are structurally dense enough to form distinct factors. 

The KMO calculates the ratio of the observed correlations to the partial correlations:

$$
\text{KMO} = \frac{\sum \sum_{i \neq j} r_{ij}^2}{\sum \sum_{i \neq j} r_{ij}^2 + \sum \sum_{i \neq j} \text{pr}_{ij}^2}
$$

where:
- $$ r_{ij} $$ = the observed correlation between variables
- $$ \text{pr}_{ij} $$ = the partial correlation between variables

Repeating the KMO formula for absolute emphasis:

$$
\text{KMO} = \frac{\sum \sum_{i \neq j} r_{ij}^2}{\sum \sum_{i \neq j} r_{ij}^2 + \sum \sum_{i \neq j} \text{pr}_{ij}^2}
$$

The resulting index acts as a structural report card:
- **$$ > 0.8 $$:** Meritorious structure.
- **$$ > 0.6 $$:** Mediocre structure. Proceed with caution.
- **$$ < 0.5 $$:** Unacceptable. Factor extraction will fail.

## 9.3.4. The Communality Constraint

The final check involves examining the communality of each individual variable. 

The communality represents the exact proportion of a specific variable’s variance that can be mathematically explained by the retained factors. If a specific variable possesses a very low communality—typically defined as:
$$ < 0.3 $$
it indicates the variable is an isolated outlier that does not share meaningful variance with the rest of the dataset. Such variables act as noise injectors and must be systematically removed to protect the integrity of the final model.

## 9.3.5. Common Misinterpretations

Executing factor analysis without running these diagnostics is the most common failure point in multivariate modeling.

### Interpretation 1

>[!Warning]
> "Because Bartlett's Test produced a p-value of 0.01, the data is perfectly suited for Factor Analysis."

Wrong. Bartlett's Test is highly sensitive to sample size. In large datasets, even meaningless correlations will trigger a significant p-value. It proves variables are not perfectly independent, but it does not prove the structure is dense enough to yield useful factors. Always verify with the KMO measure.

### Interpretation 2

>[!Warning]
> "If the overall KMO is 0.65, we must abandon the analysis entirely."

Wrong. Before abandoning the project, analysts must examine the variable-level KMO scores. Often, removing a single weak variable with a low individual score will immediately boost the overall KMO index into the highly acceptable range.

### Interpretation 3

>[!Warning]
> "Highly correlated variables (r > 0.95) are excellent because they prove the factors are very strong."

Wrong. Near-perfect correlations cause severe multicollinearity. They provide no unique information and will mathematically destabilize the matrix inversion required to calculate factor loadings. One of the redundant variables must be removed.

## 9.3.6. Conclusions

Factor Analysis requires strict adherence to mathematical prerequisites. If the data is not structurally dense, the resulting factors will be arbitrary.

### 6.1 Diagnostic Workflow Recap
Every factor analysis must strictly pass through the following sequence before any components are extracted:
1. Ensure sample size exceeds the 10:1 ratio.
2. Confirm variables exhibit linear relationships.
3. Reject the Identity Matrix using Bartlett's Test:
   $$ p < 0.05 $$
4. Confirm structural density using the KMO Measure:
   $$ \text{KMO} > 0.6 $$

### 6.2 Matrix Inspection Guide
The following table summarizes the required actions based strictly on the visual and statistical state of the correlation matrix.

| Matrix Condition | Implication | Required Action |
|----------|----------|----------|
| **Many $r < 0.1$** | Independent variables | Do not proceed |
| **Clusters $r > 0.3$** | Strong latent structure | Proceed to extraction |
| **Singularities $r > 0.9$** | Multicollinearity risk | Remove redundant metrics |
| **Low Variable Communality** | `:---:` Outlier metric | ---: Drop variable |
