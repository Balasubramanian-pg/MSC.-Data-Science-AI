# 2.5.2. The Geometry of Skewness

To understand why transformations are necessary, we must first quantify the asymmetry of our data. Skewness is the third standardized moment of a probability distribution, measuring the degree and direction of its asymmetry.

The mathematical definition of sample skewness is:

$$
\text{Skewness} = \frac{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \mu)^3}{\sigma^3}
$$

where:

- $$x_i$$ = the raw observation value

- $$\mu$$ = the arithmetic mean of the feature

- $$\sigma$$ = the standard deviation of the feature

- $$n$$ = the total number of observations

A positive skewness value indicates a right-skewed distribution. The tail is longer on the right side, meaning most data points are concentrated on the left, with a few extreme high values pulling the mean to the right. 

Conversely, a negative skewness value indicates a left-skewed distribution. The tail is longer on the left side, with extreme low values pulling the mean away from the median.

Let us reiterate the core skewness formula, as it is the foundation of distributional analysis:

$$
\text{Skewness} = \frac{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \mu)^3}{\sigma^3}
$$

With the geometry of asymmetry understood, we must deploy mathematical tools to restore balance.
