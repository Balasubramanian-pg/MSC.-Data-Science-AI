# 4.3. t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE)

## 4.3.1. The Limitation of Linear Projections

Techniques like Principal Component Analysis strictly preserve global variance by creating linear combinations of features. However, real-world data often exists on complex, non-linear manifolds. 

When high-dimensional data is inherently non-linear, linear dimensionality reduction forces structurally distant points to overlap in the projected space. To effectively visualize non-linear topologies, we require an algorithm that prioritizes local structure over global variance. t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) is specifically optimized to fulfill this visualization objective.

## 4.3.2. The Probabilistic Framework for Local Structure

t-SNE Abandons variance maximization. Instead, it operates through a probabilistic framework designed to capture pairwise neighborhood relationships.

The core intuition is that Euclidean distances in a high-dimensional space can be seamlessly converted into conditional probabilities representing similarities. If two data points are physically close in the original feature space, the probability that they are neighbors is mathematically high.

## 4.3.3. High-Dimensional Similarity

For every target data point, we compute the similarity to all other points using a Gaussian distribution centered on that target point.

The conditional probability of point:

$$
x_j
$$

being chosen as a neighbor by point:

$$
x_i
$$

is defined as:

$$
p_{j|i} = \frac{\exp\left(- \frac{\|x_i - x_j\|^2}{2\sigma_i^2}\right)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp\left(- \frac{\|x_i - x_k\|^2}{2\sigma_i^2}\right)}
$$

where:

- $$x_i$$ = original target data point
- $$x_j$$ = potential neighbor data point
- $$\sigma_i$$= variance of the Gaussian distribution centered at$$x_i$$
- $$\|x_i - x_j\|^2$$ = squared Euclidean distance between the points

Because we want a symmetric probability matrix for optimization, the final high-dimensional similarity is mathematically averaged:

$$
p_{ij} = \frac{p_{j|i} + p_{i|j}}{2N}
$$

where:

- $$N$$ = total number of data points
- $$p_{ij}$$ = symmetric high-dimensional probability

## 4.3.4. The Crowding Problem and Student's t-Distribution

When attempting to project a highly complex dataset into two dimensions, a geometric limitation known as the "crowding problem" occurs. In low-dimensional space, there is simply not enough physical area to accommodate all the moderately distant neighbors, causing distinct clusters to mathematically crush into one another.

To solve this, t-SNE maps the high-dimensional probabilities into low-dimensional counterparts using a Student's t-distribution with a single degree of freedom.

The low-dimensional similarity between points:

$$
y_i
$$

and:

$$
y_j
$$

is defined as:

$$
q_{ij} = \frac{\left(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2\right)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \neq l} \left(1 + \|y_k - y_l\|^2\right)^{-1}}
$$

where:

- $$y_i$$= low-dimensional mapping of$$x_i$$
- $$y_j$$= low-dimensional mapping of$$x_j$$

Unlike the Gaussian distribution, the Student's t-distribution possesses significantly heavier tails.

>[!Tip]
> The heavy tails of the t-distribution mathematically enforce a strong repulsive force, pushing apart points that are not immediate neighbors and preventing the crowding problem.

## 4.3.5. Optimization via Kullback-Leibler Divergence

The ultimate goal of the algorithm is to arrange the low-dimensional points so that the probability distribution matches the high-dimensional distribution as closely as possible.

This is achieved by minimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence:

$$
KL(P || Q) = \sum_{i \neq j} p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}
$$

where:

- $$P$$ = high-dimensional probability distribution
- $$Q$$ = low-dimensional probability distribution
- $$p_{ij}$$ = original affinities
- $$q_{ij}$$ = projected affinities

By repeatedly applying gradient descent to this cost function, t-SNE updates the coordinates in the low-dimensional space until a stable local minimum is reached.

For maximum emphasis on the cost function driving the entire algorithm, we restate the optimization objective:

$$
KL(P || Q) = \sum_{i \neq j} p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}
$$

## 4.3.6. Example of a t-SNE Optimization Workflow

Suppose:
- Original high-dimensional feature set contains handwritten digits.
- Number of observations is 1000.
- Original dimensionality is 784.
- Desired low dimensionality is 2.
- The hyperparameter Perplexity (number of nearest neighbors to balance) is set to 30.

### Step 1: Pre-process via Principal Component Analysis
Reduce the original 784 dimensions to 50 dimensions to aggressively cut computational complexity from the distance calculations.

### Step 2: Compute High-Dimensional Affinities
Calculate all symmetric probabilities for the 50-dimensional space using:

$$
p_{ij} = \frac{p_{j|i} + p_{i|j}}{2000}
$$

### Step 3: Initialize Low-Dimensional Coordinates
Randomly assign initial 2D coordinates for all 1000 observations using a standard normal distribution.

### Step 4: Compute Low-Dimensional Affinities
Calculate probabilities in the 2D space utilizing the heavy-tailed t-distribution equation:

$$
q_{ij} = \frac{\left(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2\right)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \neq l} \left(1 + \|y_k - y_l\|^2\right)^{-1}}
$$

### Step 5: Execute Gradient Descent
Iteratively shift the 2D coordinates to minimize the Kullback-Leibler divergence until the mapped clusters visually separate.

## 4.3.7. Strategic Usage: Visualization vs. Feature Engineering

It is mathematically critical to distinguish t-SNE from classical feature extraction methodologies.

t-SNE is strictly designed for exploratory data visualization. It assists practitioners in rapidly identifying hidden clusters, confirming grouping similarities, and isolating potential anomalies.

However, t-SNE is profoundly unsuited for feature engineering in predictive modeling. The cost function optimization relies heavily on a stochastic initialization. Consequently, executing the algorithm multiple times on the exact same dataset will generate radically different coordinate mappings. Because there is no generalized algebraic projection matrix, the transformation cannot be mapped to unseen out-of-sample data.

## 4.3.8. Comparing t-SNE and PCA

The following table explicitly details the structural and strategic differences between these two prominent dimensionality reduction algorithms.

| Feature | Principal Component Analysis | t-SNE |
|---|:---:|---:|
| **Mathematical Objective** | Preserve global variance | Preserve local neighborhoods |
| **Mapping Geometry** | Strictly linear combinations | Highly complex non-linear |
| **Output Stability** | Completely deterministic | Highly stochastic |
| **Predictive Utility** | Serves as robust model input | None (Cannot transform new data) |

## 4.3.9. Common Misinterpretations

Because the resulting visual clusters are often aesthetically pleasing, practitioners frequently draw mathematically invalid conclusions from t-SNE output plots.

### 9.1 Interpretation 1

>[!Warning]
> "The physical distance between two distinct clusters in a t-SNE plot represents their actual distance in the original feature space."

Wrong.
t-SNE preserves local neighborhoods, not global geometry. The space between completely separate clusters is warped by the algorithm and holds almost no meaningful absolute distance metric.

### 9.2 Interpretation 2

>[!Warning]
> "The physical size or density of a t-SNE cluster reflects the variance of that group in the original data."

Wrong.
The algorithm actively adapts to the regional density of the data, mathematically expanding dense clusters and compressing sparse ones. Visual density in the plot is heavily distorted.

### 9.3 Interpretation 3

>[!Warning]
> "I can use the 2D t-SNE output as input features to train my random forest classifier."

Wrong.
Because t-SNE is a stochastic mapping without a persistent transformation matrix, it is impossible to transform future testing data into the exact same spatial coordinates. It must never be used in a predictive pipeline.

## 4.3.10. Conclusions

t-SNE provides an unparalleled mathematical lens for visualizing the local topology of highly complex, non-linear data structures, specifically resolving the failures of linear projection techniques.

### 10.1 Anatomy of the Manifold Mapping

The success of the algorithm fundamentally relies on the contrast between two distinct probability distributions:

- **High-Dimensional Space:** Utilizes standard Gaussian distributions to quantify initial similarities.
- **Low-Dimensional Space:** Utilizes the heavy-tailed Student's t-distribution to actively prevent crowding.

### 10.2 Optimization Engine

The spatial arrangement of the final visualization is solely governed by the minimization of the informational discrepancy:

$$
KL(P || Q) = \sum_{i \neq j} p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}
$$

>[!Note]
> Always view t-SNE exclusively as an exploratory diagnostic tool. It illuminates structural patterns for the human eye, but provides no persistent algebraic features for machine learning pipelines.
