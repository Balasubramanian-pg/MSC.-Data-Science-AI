# 6.2. Corner Detection

## 6.2.1. From Edges to Points of Interest

Edge detection identifies boundaries, but boundaries alone do not fully describe an image's structure.

A corner is a point where two or more edges intersect. Unlike an edge pixel, which exhibits intensity variation in only one direction, a corner exhibits significant intensity variation in **multiple directions**. This multi-directional variation makes corners uniquely informative for tasks like image matching, object tracking, and scene reconstruction.

The intensity at a pixel $$(x, y)$$ in a grayscale image is:

$$
I(x, y), \quad x = 1, 2, \dots, W; \quad y = 1, 2, \dots, H
$$

where:

- $$I(x, y)$$= intensity at pixel coordinate$$(x, y)$$

- $$W$$ = image width in pixels

- $$H$$ = image height in pixels

An edge is a locus of points where the gradient magnitude is locally maximal in one direction. A corner is a point where the gradient is large in at least two independent directions. The fundamental difference is directional variation.

>[!Note]
> Corners are more informative than edges for many vision tasks because they are localizable, distinctive, and invariant to small viewpoint changes.

## 6.2.2. Why Edge Detection Alone Is Fundamentally Incomplete

Suppose two algorithms analyze the same image.

- Algorithm A extracts only edge maps

- Algorithm B extracts both edges and corner points

Algorithm B can establish point correspondences between images; Algorithm A cannot.

This happens because edge points are ambiguous under translation. A point on a straight edge could correspond to infinitely many points on a matching edge in another image. The key insight is:

>[!Note]
> An edge point is not a unique landmark. A corner point is. The intersection of edges creates a distinctive feature that can be matched across images.

That means the neighborhood around a corner has its own characteristic structure tensor that distinguishes it from both flat regions and edge points. The local intensity pattern is the signal.

## 6.2.3. Harris Corner Detection

Harris Corner Detection formalizes the intuition that corners produce large intensity changes when a small window is shifted in any direction.

### 3.1 The Intuition

Consider a small window $$W$$centered at pixel$$(x, y)$$. Shift this window by a small amount $$(\Delta x, \Delta y)$$. The change in intensity is:

$$
E(\Delta x, \Delta y) = \sum_{(u,v) \in W} w(u,v) \left[ I(u+\Delta x, v+\Delta y) - I(u,v) \right]^2
$$

where:

- $$E(\Delta x, \Delta y)$$ = sum of squared intensity differences after shift

- $$w(u,v)$$ = window weight (typically Gaussian or uniform)

- $$I(u,v)$$= intensity at pixel$$(u,v)$$ within the window

For small shifts, we approximate using a first-order Taylor expansion:

$$
I(u+\Delta x, v+\Delta y) \approx I(u,v) + I_x(u,v)\Delta x + I_y(u,v)\Delta y
$$

where:

- $$I_x(u,v)$$= partial derivative of intensity with respect to$$x$$

- $$I_y(u,v)$$= partial derivative of intensity with respect to$$y$$

Substituting:

$$
E(\Delta x, \Delta y) \approx \sum_{(u,v) \in W} w(u,v) \left[ I_x(u,v)\Delta x + I_y(u,v)\Delta y \right]^2
$$

This can be written in matrix form:

$$
E(\Delta x, \Delta y) \approx \begin{bmatrix} \Delta x & \Delta y \end{bmatrix} M \begin{bmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta y \end{bmatrix}
$$

where $$M$$ is the **structure tensor** (autocorrelation matrix):

$$
M = \sum_{(u,v) \in W} w(u,v) \begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_x I_y \\ I_x I_y & I_y^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A & C \\ C & B \end{bmatrix}
$$

where:

- $$A = \sum w(u,v) I_x^2$$ = sum of squared horizontal gradients

- $$B = \sum w(u,v) I_y^2$$ = sum of squared vertical gradients

- $$C = \sum w(u,v) I_x I_y$$ = sum of cross-gradient products

### 3.2 Interpreting the Structure Tensor

The eigenvalues of $$M$$ reveal the local structure:

| Eigenvalue Pattern | Interpretation | Visual Category |
|---|---|---|
| $$\lambda_1 \approx 0, \lambda_2 \approx 0$$ | No intensity change in any direction | Flat region |
| $$\lambda_1 \gg 0, \lambda_2 \approx 0$$ | Large change in one direction only | Edge |
| $$\lambda_1 \gg 0, \lambda_2 \gg 0$$ | Large change in both directions | Corner |

where:

- $$\lambda_1, \lambda_2$$= eigenvalues of the structure tensor$$M$$

- $$\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq 0$$(both eigenvalues are non-negative because$$M$$ is symmetric positive semi-definite)

>[!Note]
> The structure tensor transforms the problem of corner detection into an eigenvalue analysis. A corner is defined mathematically as a point where both eigenvalues are large.

### 3.3 The Harris Response Function

Rather than computing eigenvalues explicitly (computationally expensive), Harris proposed a response function:

$$
R = \det(M) - k \cdot \text{tr}(M)^2
$$

where:

- $$R$$ = Harris corner response score

- $$\det(M) = AB - C^2 = \lambda_1 \lambda_2$$= determinant of$$M$$

- $$\text{tr}(M) = A + B = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$$= trace of$$M$$

- $$k$$= empirical constant (typically$$0.04$$ to $$0.06$$)

The response $$R$$ classifies local structure:

| Response Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| $$R \gg 0$$ | Corner (both eigenvalues large) |
| $$R \ll 0$$ | Edge (one eigenvalue large, one small) |
| $$|R| \approx 0$$ | Flat region (both eigenvalues small) |

### 3.4 Algorithm Steps

The Harris algorithm proceeds through five steps:

**Step 1: Compute Image Gradients**

$$
I_x = \frac{\partial I}{\partial x}, \quad I_y = \frac{\partial I}{\partial y}
$$

using Sobel operators or finite differences.

**Step 2: Build the Structure Tensor**

At each pixel, compute:

$$
M = \begin{bmatrix} \sum w I_x^2 & \sum w I_x I_y \\ \sum w I_x I_y & \sum w I_y^2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

where the sums are over the local window $$W$$.

**Step 3: Calculate Corner Response**

$$
R = \det(M) - k \cdot \text{tr}(M)^2
$$

**Step 4: Threshold the Response**

Identify candidate corners where:

$$
R > T
$$

where $$T$$is a threshold (often expressed as a fraction of the maximum response, e.g.,$$T = 0.01 \cdot R_{\max}$$).

**Step 5: Apply Non-Maximum Suppression**

Within each local neighborhood, retain only the pixel with maximum $$R$$. Suppress all other candidates.

## 6.2.4. Shi-Tomasi Corner Detection

Shi-Tomasi improves upon Harris by using a more direct and stable criterion.

### 4.1 The Minimum Eigenvalue Criterion

Instead of the Harris response formula, Shi-Tomasi uses:

$$
R_{\text{ST}} = \min(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)
$$

where:

- $$R_{\text{ST}}$$ = Shi-Tomasi corner response

- $$\lambda_1, \lambda_2$$ = eigenvalues of the structure tensor $$M$$

- $$\min(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)$$ = the smaller eigenvalue

A corner is confirmed if and only if:

$$
\min(\lambda_1, \lambda_2) > T
$$

where $$T$$ is a threshold.

### 4.2 Why Shi-Tomasi Is More Robust

The Harris response $$R = \det(M) - k \cdot \text{tr}(M)^2$$ depends on the empirical constant $$k$$. The choice of $$k$$ affects the balance between corner and edge classification.

Shi-Tomasi eliminates this parameter entirely. By using $$\min(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)$$, the algorithm directly tests the defining property of a corner: both eigenvalues must be large. The smaller eigenvalue is the bottleneck; if it is large, the point is unambiguously a corner.

>[!Tip]
> Shi-Tomasi is generally preferred in modern pipelines because it is less sensitive to parameter tuning and produces more repeatable detections across similar images.

## 6.2.5. Example: Computing Harris and Shi-Tomasi Responses

Suppose:

- A $$3 \times 3$$ window centered at pixel $$(x, y)$$

- Gradient values within the window:

| $$I_x$$ | $$I_y$$ |
|---|---|
| 2 | 1 |
| 3 | 2 |
| 1 | 1 |
| 2 | 3 |
| 4 | 2 |
| 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 1 |
| 3 | 3 |
| 2 | 2 |

### Step 1: Compute Structure Tensor Components

With uniform weights ($$w = 1$$ for all pixels):

$$
A = \sum I_x^2 = 4 + 9 + 1 + 4 + 16 + 1 + 4 + 9 + 4 = 52
$$

$$
B = \sum I_y^2 = 1 + 4 + 1 + 9 + 4 + 4 + 1 + 9 + 4 = 37
$$

$$
C = \sum I_x I_y = 2 + 6 + 1 + 6 + 8 + 2 + 2 + 9 + 4 = 40
$$

### Step 2: Form the Structure Tensor

$$
M = \begin{bmatrix} 52 & 40 \\ 40 & 37 \end{bmatrix}
$$

### Step 3: Compute Eigenvalues

The characteristic equation is:

$$
\det(M - \lambda I) = (52 - \lambda)(37 - \lambda) - 40^2 = 0
$$

$$
\lambda^2 - 89\lambda + 1924 - 1600 = 0
$$

$$
\lambda^2 - 89\lambda + 324 = 0
$$

Using the quadratic formula:

$$
\lambda = \frac{89 \pm \sqrt{89^2 - 4(324)}}{2} = \frac{89 \pm \sqrt{7921 - 1296}}{2} = \frac{89 \pm \sqrt{6625}}{2}
$$

$$
\lambda = \frac{89 \pm 81.39}{2}
$$

$$
\lambda_1 = \frac{89 + 81.39}{2} = 85.20, \quad \lambda_2 = \frac{89 - 81.39}{2} = 3.81
$$

### Step 4: Compute Harris Response

With $$k = 0.04$$:

$$
\det(M) = 52 \times 37 - 40^2 = 1924 - 1600 = 324
$$

$$
\text{tr}(M) = 52 + 37 = 89
$$

$$
R = 324 - 0.04 \times 89^2 = 324 - 0.04 \times 7921 = 324 - 316.84 = 7.16
$$

Since $$R > 0$$, this point is classified as a corner.

### Step 5: Compute Shi-Tomasi Response

$$
R_{\text{ST}} = \min(\lambda_1, \lambda_2) = \min(85.20, 3.81) = 3.81
$$

Since both eigenvalues are positive and the minimum is non-negligible, Shi-Tomasi also classifies this as a corner. The small value of $$\lambda_2$$ relative to $$\lambda_1$$ suggests a weak corner or an edge-like structure.

## 6.2.6. Practical Implementation Parameters

### 6.1 Harris Corner Detection Parameters

| Parameter | Symbol | Description | Typical Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Block size | — | Size of neighborhood for structure tensor | 2 to 7 pixels |
| Sobel kernel size | $$k_{\text{size}}$$ | Aperture for gradient computation | 3 or 5 |
| Harris constant | $$k$$ | Empirical parameter in response formula | 0.04 to 0.06 |

### 6.2 Shi-Tomasi Corner Detection Parameters

| Parameter | Symbol | Description | Typical Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maximum corners | $$N_{\max}$$ | Upper limit on detected corners | 25 to 100 |
| Quality level | $$q$$ | Minimum acceptable response as fraction of maximum | 0.01 to 0.10 |
| Minimum distance | $$d_{\min}$$ | Minimum Euclidean distance between corners | 5 to 20 pixels |

The minimum distance parameter is particularly important. It prevents clustered detections by enforcing spatial separation:

$$
\|\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j\|_2 \geq d_{\min}, \quad \forall i \neq j
$$

where $$\mathbf{p}_i = (x_i, y_i)$$ is the coordinate of the $$i$$-th detected corner.

## 6.2.7. Comparison of Harris and Shi-Tomasi

| Feature | Harris Corner Detection | Shi-Tomasi Corner Detection |
|---|---|---|
| **Response metric** | $$\det(M) - k \cdot \text{tr}(M)^2$$ | $$\min(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)$$ |
| **Parameter sensitivity** | Highly dependent on $$k$$ | No empirical constant required |
| **Stability** | Good | Better (more repeatable) |
| **Computational cost** | Lower (no eigenvalue decomposition) | Higher (requires eigenvalues) |
| **Common API** | `cv2.cornerHarris()` | `cv2.goodFeaturesToTrack()` |
| **Modern preference** | Classic, well-understood | Generally preferred |

## 6.2.8. Common Misinterpretations

### Interpretation 1

>[!Warning]
> "A high Harris response always indicates a strong corner."

Wrong. The Harris response can be positive for edge-like structures with moderately large eigenvalues. Shi-Tomasi's minimum eigenvalue criterion is more discriminating because it requires both eigenvalues to be large.

### Interpretation 2

>[!Warning]
> "More detected corners always improve matching performance."

Wrong. Excessive corners increase computational cost and may include unstable points from texture or noise. The quality level and minimum distance parameters should be tuned to retain only the most salient and spatially distributed corners.

### Interpretation 3

>[!Warning]
> "Corner detection is rotation invariant."

Wrong. While corners are more robust to rotation than edges, the exact response values and detected positions may shift under rotation due to discretization effects. For true rotation invariance, additional descriptors (like SIFT or ORB) are required.

## 6.2.9. Conclusions

Corner detection transforms raw pixel intensities into sparse, distinctive landmarks that enable image correspondence, tracking, and reconstruction.

### 9.1 Anatomy of Corner Detection

The structure of every corner detection algorithm follows:

$$
\text{Corner Map} = \text{Gradient} \rightarrow \text{Structure Tensor} \rightarrow \text{Response} \rightarrow \text{Threshold} \rightarrow \text{Non-Maximum Suppression}
$$

- **Gradient**: Compute local intensity derivatives $$I_x$$ and $$I_y$$

- **Structure Tensor**: Form the autocorrelation matrix $$M$$ from gradient products

- **Response**: Compute corner strength (Harris or Shi-Tomasi criterion)

- **Threshold**: Retain points exceeding a minimum response

- **Non-Maximum Suppression**: Ensure spatial uniqueness of detections

### 9.2 Algorithm Selection Guide

| Scenario | Recommended Method | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Research or educational use | Harris | Classic formulation, well-understood theory |
| Production object tracking | Shi-Tomasi | More stable, better parameter control |
| Real-time applications | Shi-Tomasi with `minDistance` | Avoids clustered detections, reduces matching cost |
| Image stitching | Shi-Tomasi | Repeatability across viewpoint changes |

### 9.3 Critical Principles

Understanding the geometric nature of corners is essential:

- **Corners are localizable**: Unlike edge points, corners have unique 2D position

- **Corners are distinctive**: The multi-directional intensity variation creates signatures matchable across images

- **Corners are sparse**: Proper parameter tuning yields a manageable set of high-quality landmarks

>[!Tip]
> "The best corner detectors combine gradient sensitivity with geometric discrimination. Harris provided the theoretical foundation; Shi-Tomasi refined the criterion for practical robustness."

You are now equipped with the technical foundation to detect corners in digital images using both classical and modern approaches. The next step is leveraging these corner points for higher-level vision tasks such as feature matching, image stitching, and object tracking.
