# 7.2. Time Series Analysis in Finance

## 7.2.1. From Raw Prices to Market Intelligence

Financial markets generate vast quantities of raw data.

Every trading day produces an OHLCV record: Open, High, Low, Close, and Volume. A single stock observed over one year yields approximately 252 rows of these five columns. In isolation, each number describes a moment. Collectively, they describe a stochastic process evolving through time.

The raw close price at time $$t$$ is:

$$
P_t
$$

where:

- $$P_t$$ = closing price at time $$t$$

- $$t = 1, 2, \dots, T$$

This raw price is a **point estimate** of value at a single instant. It contains no memory, no context, and no measure of uncertainty. A model trained on raw prices alone would miss the temporal structure that defines financial markets.

The solution is **domain-informed feature engineering**: transforming raw OHLCV data into indicators that capture momentum, volatility, and trend direction.

## 7.2.2. Why Raw Price Data Is Fundamentally Incomplete

Suppose two traders analyze the same stock.

- Trader A uses only today's closing price to predict tomorrow's movement

- Trader B uses a 14-day moving average, recent volatility, and momentum indicators

Trader B's model will almost certainly outperform Trader A's.

This happens because financial prices exhibit **temporal dependency**, **volatility clustering**, and **mean-reverting tendencies**. The key insight is:

>[!Note]
> A stock price is not an independent random draw. It is a realization of a stochastic process where past behavior conditions future distributions.

That means the price at time $$t$$ has its own probability structure conditional on history. The past directly influences the present through investor memory, market sentiment, and autocorrelated order flow.

## 7.2.3. Foundational Engineering Toolkit

Before applying domain-specific indicators, we construct a base layer of temporal features from OHLCV data.

### 3.1 Lag Features

Lag features provide the model with explicit memory of recent prices:

$$
P_{t-k}^{\text{(lag)}} = P_{t-k}
$$

where:

- $$k$$ = lag length (typically 1, 3, or 7 trading days)

- $$P_{t-k}$$ = price at time $$t-k$$

Common lag choices for daily financial data:

| Lag | Description | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| $$t-1$$ | Previous close | Captures immediate momentum |
| $$t-3$$ | Three days prior | Captures short-term reversal |
| $$t-7$$ | One week prior | Captures weekly cyclicality |

### 3.2 Rolling Statistics

Rolling statistics summarize local behavior within a moving window of $$w$$ periods.

The rolling mean filters noise to reveal the local trend:

$$
\bar{P}_t^{(w)} = \frac{1}{w} \sum_{i=0}^{w-1} P_{t-i}
$$

where:

- $$w$$ = window size (e.g., 7 or 14 trading days)

- $$P_{t-i}$$ = price at time $$t-i$$

The rolling standard deviation quantifies local volatility:

$$
\sigma_t^{(w)} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{w-1} \sum_{i=0}^{w-1} \left(P_{t-i} - \bar{P}_t^{(w)}\right)^2}
$$

where:

- $$\sigma_t^{(w)}$$ = rolling standard deviation at time $$t$$

- $$\bar{P}_t^{(w)}$$ = rolling mean at time $$t$$

### 3.3 Trend and Slope Features

Fitting a linear regression within a rolling window captures the local rate of price change. For window $$w$$:

$$
P_{t-i} = \beta_{0t} + \beta_{1t}(t-i) + \epsilon_{t-i}, \quad i = 0, 1, \dots, w-1
$$

The slope coefficient $$\beta_{1t}$$ represents the local trend direction:

- $$\beta_{1t} > 0$$ indicates upward price momentum

- $$\beta_{1t} < 0$$ indicates downward price momentum

- $$\beta_{1t} \approx 0$$ indicates consolidation or sideways movement

This is more informative than the rolling mean alone because it distinguishes between levels and directions of change.

## 7.2.4. Domain-Specific Indicators

### 4.1 Moving Average

The simple moving average (SMA) over $$w$$ periods smooths price paths to reveal the underlying trend:

$$
\text{SMA}_t^{(w)} = \frac{1}{w} \sum_{i=0}^{w-1} P_{t-i}
$$

where:

- $$w$$ = window size (commonly 14, 50, or 200 trading days)

- $$P_{t-i}$$ = closing price at time $$t-i$$

The 14-day SMA is particularly useful for short-to-medium term trend identification. It represents the "fair value" around which prices oscillate in the near term.

### 4.2 Relative Strength Index (RSI)

RSI is a momentum oscillator measuring the speed and magnitude of price movements on a scale of 0 to 100.

#### 4.2.1 Price Differences

First, compute the price change at each time step:

$$
\Delta_t = P_t - P_{t-1}
$$

where:

- $$\Delta_t$$ = price change at time $$t$$

- $$P_t$$ = closing price at time $$t$$

- $$P_{t-1}$$ = closing price at time $$t-1$$

#### 4.2.2 Gain and Loss Streams

Separate price changes into gains and losses:

$$
G_t = \begin{cases} \Delta_t & \text{if } \Delta_t > 0 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

$$
L_t = \begin{cases} |\Delta_t| & \text{if } \Delta_t < 0 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

where:

- $$G_t$$ = gain at time $$t$$

- $$L_t$$ = loss at time $$t$$

#### 4.2.3 Average Gain and Average Loss

Compute rolling averages over window $$w$$:

$$
\text{AvgGain}_t^{(w)} = \frac{1}{w} \sum_{i=0}^{w-1} G_{t-i}
$$

$$
\text{AvgLoss}_t^{(w)} = \frac{1}{w} \sum_{i=0}^{w-1} L_{t-i}
$$

#### 4.2.4 Relative Strength and RSI

$$
\text{RS}_t = \frac{\text{AvgGain}_t^{(w)}}{\text{AvgLoss}_t^{(w)}}
$$

$$
\text{RSI}_t = 100 - \frac{100}{1 + \text{RS}_t}
$$

where:

- $$\text{RS}_t$$ = relative strength at time $$t$$

- $$\text{RSI}_t$$ = relative strength index at time $$t$$

- $$w$$ = lookback window (typically 14 periods)

Interpretation thresholds:

| RSI Value | Market Condition | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| RSI > 70 | Overbought | Potential price pullback |
| RSI < 30 | Oversold | Potential price rebound |
| 30 ≤ RSI ≤ 70 | Neutral | No strong momentum signal |

>[!Note]
> RSI measures momentum, not direction. A high RSI indicates strong recent buying pressure; a low RSI indicates strong recent selling pressure.

### 4.3 Bollinger Bands

Bollinger Bands visualize volatility by plotting a moving average plus and minus two standard deviations. They define a dynamic range of "normal" market behavior.

#### 4.3.1 Band Construction

$$
\text{Upper Band}_t = \text{SMA}_t^{(w)} + 2\sigma_t^{(w)}
$$

$$
\text{Lower Band}_t = \text{SMA}_t^{(w)} - 2\sigma_t^{(w)}
$$

where:

- $$\text{SMA}_t^{(w)}$$ = simple moving average at time $$t$$

- $$\sigma_t^{(w)}$$ = rolling standard deviation at time $$t$$

- $$w$$ = window size (typically 20 periods)

#### 4.3.2 Band Width and Position

The band width quantifies volatility:

$$
\text{Band Width}_t = \text{Upper Band}_t - \text{Lower Band}_t = 4\sigma_t^{(w)}
$$

The relative position of price within the bands:

$$
\%B_t = \frac{P_t - \text{Lower Band}_t}{\text{Upper Band}_t - \text{Lower Band}_t}
$$

where:

- $$\%B_t$$ = percent bandwidth at time $$t$$

- $$\%B_t > 1$$ indicates price above the upper band (unusual strength)

- $$\%B_t < 0$$ indicates price below the lower band (unusual weakness)

## 7.2.5. Example: Computing RSI and Bollinger Bands

Suppose:

- Closing prices for 20 consecutive trading days are available

- Window size for RSI: $$w = 14$$

- Window size for Bollinger Bands: $$w = 20$$

### Step 1: Compute Price Differences

$$
\Delta_t = P_t - P_{t-1}, \quad t = 2, 3, \dots, 20
$$

### Step 2: Separate Gains and Losses

For each $$\Delta_t$$:

$$
G_t = \max(\Delta_t, 0), \quad L_t = \max(-\Delta_t, 0)
$$

### Step 3: Compute Average Gain and Average Loss

At $$t = 15$$ (first point with 14 prior differences):

$$
\text{AvgGain}_{15}^{(14)} = \frac{1}{14} \sum_{i=2}^{15} G_i
$$

$$
\text{AvgLoss}_{15}^{(14)} = \frac{1}{14} \sum_{i=2}^{15} L_i
$$

### Step 4: Compute Relative Strength and RSI

$$
\text{RS}_{15} = \frac{\text{AvgGain}_{15}^{(14)}}{\text{AvgLoss}_{15}^{(14)}}
$$

$$
\text{RSI}_{15} = 100 - \frac{100}{1 + \text{RS}_{15}}
$$

### Step 5: Compute Bollinger Bands

At $$t = 20$$ (first point with 20 prior closes):

$$
\text{SMA}_{20}^{(20)} = \frac{1}{20} \sum_{i=1}^{20} P_i
$$

$$
\sigma_{20}^{(20)} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{19} \sum_{i=1}^{20} \left(P_i - \text{SMA}_{20}^{(20)}\right)^2}
$$

$$
\text{Upper Band}_{20} = \text{SMA}_{20}^{(20)} + 2\sigma_{20}^{(20)}
$$

$$
\text{Lower Band}_{20} = \text{SMA}_{20}^{(20)} - 2\sigma_{20}^{(20)}
$$

Final indicators at $$t = 20$$:

- $$\text{RSI}_{20}$$ (if 14 prior differences available)

- $$\text{Upper Band}_{20}$$

- $$\text{Lower Band}_{20}$$

## 7.2.6. Critical Implementation Trade-offs

### 6.1 Data Leakage Prevention

>[!Warning]
> Never use a centered window when constructing features for time $$t$$. For a 14-day RSI, only data from times $$t-14$$ through $$t-1$$ may be used. Using data at time $$t$$ to construct a feature for time $$t$$ is a classic error that produces artificially high model accuracy.

Mathematically, the valid feature at time $$t$$ must satisfy:

$$
\text{Feature}_t = f(P_{t-w}, P_{t-w+1}, \dots, P_{t-1})
$$

where $$f(\cdot)$$ is the aggregation function. The index $$t$$ must never appear on the right-hand side.

### 6.2 Stationarity Concerns

Raw stock prices are almost never stationary. They exhibit trends, drift, and changing variance. While technical indicators help, differencing may still be necessary:

$$
\Delta P_t = P_t - P_{t-1}
$$

or for percentage returns:

$$
r_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} \times 100
$$

where:

- $$r_t$$ = simple return at time $$t$$

- $$\Delta P_t$$ = first difference of price at time $$t$$

Differencing stabilizes the mean and is often required before applying models that assume stationarity.

### 6.3 Dimensionality vs. Signal

Adding indicators increases the feature space. But correlated indicators add noise rather than signal. The correlation between two features $$X_i$$ and $$X_j$$ is:

$$
\rho_{ij} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j)}{\sigma_{X_i} \sigma_{X_j}}
$$

If $$\rho_{ij}$$ exceeds a threshold (e.g., $$0.85$$), one feature is redundant. Use time-series cross-validation to verify that each indicator improves predictive accuracy.

## 7.2.7. Factors Affecting Indicator Quality

### 7.1 Window Size Selection

The choice of $$w$$ profoundly affects indicator behavior:

| Window Size | Effect | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Small $$w$$ (e.g., 7) | Responsive, noisy | Short-term trading signals |
| Medium $$w$$ (e.g., 14) | Balanced | General momentum analysis |
| Large $$w$$ (e.g., 50, 200) | Smooth, lagged | Long-term trend identification |

### 7.2 Volatility Regime

Indicators behave differently in high-volatility versus low-volatility regimes. Bollinger Bands adapt automatically because:

$$
\text{Band Width}_t = 4\sigma_t^{(w)}
$$

When volatility increases, bands widen; when volatility decreases, bands narrow. This self-adjusting property is a key advantage over fixed-threshold indicators.

### 7.3 Market Microstructure

High-frequency data introduces microstructure noise (bid-ask bounce, discrete prices). For intraday features, consider using volume-weighted prices or median prices rather than simple closes to reduce noise.

## 7.2.8. Common Misinterpretations

### Interpretation 1

>[!Warning]
> "RSI above 70 guarantees a price reversal."

Wrong. RSI indicates overbought conditions but does not predict timing. Prices can remain overbought for extended periods in strong trends. RSI is a condition indicator, not a timing signal.

### Interpretation 2

>[!Warning]
> "Bollinger Band touches always signal entry points."

Wrong. Prices can "walk the band" during sustained trends. Touching the upper band in an uptrend often indicates continuation, not reversal. Context from trend direction and volume is essential.

### Interpretation 3

>[!Warning]
> "More indicators always improve model performance."

Wrong. Highly correlated indicators introduce multicollinearity without adding independent signal. The marginal information gain of each additional feature should be evaluated through cross-validated performance.

## 7.2.9. Conclusions

Domain-informed feature engineering transforms raw OHLCV data into structured indicators that capture market psychology, momentum, and volatility.

### 9.1 Anatomy of Financial Feature Engineering

The structure of every financial feature set follows:

$$
\text{Feature Vector}_t = \left[ \text{Lags}_t,\ \text{Rolling}_t,\ \text{Momentum}_t,\ \text{Volatility}_t \right]
$$

- **Lag features**: Encode recent price memory

- **Rolling features**: Summarize local trend and volatility

- **Momentum features** (RSI): Capture speed and magnitude of price changes

- **Volatility features** (Bollinger Bands): Define dynamic ranges of normal behavior

### 9.2 Indicator Comparison

| Indicator | Type | Primary Signal | Key Parameter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moving Average | Trend | Local fair value | Window size $$w$$ |
| RSI | Momentum | Overbought/oversold | Window size $$w$$ |
| Bollinger Bands | Volatility | Normal price range | Window size $$w$$ |

### 9.3 Critical Principles

Understanding the temporal and statistical nature of financial data is essential:

- **Leakage destroys validity**: Features must use only past data relative to the prediction point

- **Stationarity matters**: Non-stationary series require transformation before modeling

- **Parsimony protects**: Correlated indicators waste capacity and increase variance

>[!Tip]
> "The best financial models combine statistical rigor with market intuition. Moving averages reveal what prices have done; RSI reveals what momentum implies; Bollinger Bands reveal what volatility permits."

You are now equipped with the technical foundation to engineer domain-specific features from financial time series data. The next step is implementing rigorous time-series cross-validation to ensure your indicators generalize to future, unseen market conditions.
