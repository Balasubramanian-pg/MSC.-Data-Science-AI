# 7.3. Time Series Analysis in Medical Data

## 7.3.1. From Raw Signals to Clinical Insight

Physiological monitoring generates continuous streams of biological data.

An electrocardiogram (ECG) records the electrical activity of the heart over time. A single lead sampled at 360 Hz produces 360 data points per second, or over 31 million points per day. In isolation, each voltage reading describes a microsecond of electrical potential. Collectively, they describe the cardiac rhythm governing life itself.

The raw ECG voltage at time $$t$$ is:

$$
V_t
$$

where:

- $$V_t$$ = electrical potential at time $$t$$

- $$t = 1, 2, \dots, T$$

This raw voltage is a **point measurement** of electrical activity at a single instant. It contains no rhythm information, no variability measure, and no clinical context. A model trained on raw voltages alone would miss the temporal structure that defines cardiac health.

The solution is **domain-informed feature engineering**: transforming raw ECG signals into metrics that capture heart rate variability, autonomic balance, and arrhythmia indicators.

## 7.3.2. Why Raw Voltage Data Is Fundamentally Incomplete

Suppose two clinicians analyze the same patient's ECG.

- Clinician A uses only the average heart rate over 24 hours

- Clinician B examines beat-to-beat variability, trend slopes, and temporal context around each abnormal beat

Clinician B's assessment will almost certainly detect pathologies that Clinician A misses.

This happens because cardiac rhythm exhibits **temporal dependency**, **autonomic modulation**, and **contextual irregularity**. The key insight is:

>[!Note]
> A heart rate is not a single number. It is a stochastic process where the interval between beats carries diagnostic information about autonomic nervous system function.

That means the interval between consecutive beats has its own probability distribution conditional on physiological state. The variability itself is the signal.

## 7.3.3. The Core Metric: RR Intervals and Heart Rate Variability

### 3.1 RR Interval

The RR interval is the fundamental unit of cardiac time series analysis. It measures the time elapsed between two successive R-peaks in the ECG waveform:

$$
\text{RR}_i = t_{R_{i+1}} - t_{R_i}
$$

where:

- $$t_{R_i}$$ = timestamp of the $$i$$-th R-peak

- $$\text{RR}_i$$ = interval between peak $$i$$ and peak $$i+1$$

- $$i = 1, 2, \dots, N-1$$

Heart rate in beats per minute is derived directly:

$$
\text{HR}_i = \frac{60,000}{\text{RR}_i \text{ (in milliseconds)}}
$$

where:

- $$\text{HR}_i$$ = instantaneous heart rate corresponding to the $$i$$-th interval

- $$60,000$$ = milliseconds per minute

### 3.2 Heart Rate Variability (HRV)

Heart Rate Variability is the fluctuation in the duration of consecutive RR intervals:

$$
\text{HRV} = \text{Variability}(\text{RR}_1, \text{RR}_2, \dots, \text{RR}_{N-1})
$$

where the variability can be quantified through multiple statistical measures.

The clinical significance of HRV is profound:

| HRV Level | Clinical Interpretation | Physiological Implication |
|---|---|---|
| High HRV | Healthy, responsive cardiovascular system | Good autonomic balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic responses |
| Low HRV | Chronic stress, fatigue, or pathology | Autonomic dysfunction or impaired cardiac regulation |

>[!Note]
> While average heart rate gives a general picture of fitness, HRV provides a deeper look at the balance of the autonomic nervous system. The variability itself is diagnostically more informative than the mean.

## 7.3.4. Temporal and Statistical Features

### 4.1 Lag Features

Lag features capture short-term memory in heart rhythm:

$$
\text{RR}_{i-k}^{\text{(lag)}} = \text{RR}_{i-k}
$$

where:

- $$k$$ = lag length (typically 1, 3, or 7 beats)

- $$\text{RR}_{i-k}$$ = RR interval at position $$i-k$$

Common lag choices for beat-to-beat analysis:

| Lag | Description | Clinical Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| $$i-1$$ | Previous interval | Captures immediate beat-to-beat influence |
| $$i-3$$ | Three beats prior | Captures short-term respiratory modulation |
| $$i-7$$ | Seven beats prior | Captures longer autonomic cycles |

### 4.2 Rolling Statistics

Rolling statistics summarize local cardiac behavior within a window of $$w$$ consecutive beats.

The rolling mean captures local trend:

$$
\overline{\text{RR}}_i^{(w)} = \frac{1}{w} \sum_{j=0}^{w-1} \text{RR}_{i-j}
$$

where:

- $$w$$ = window size (e.g., 7 or 15 beats)

- $$\text{RR}_{i-j}$$ = RR interval at position $$i-j$$

The rolling standard deviation captures local variability:

$$
\sigma_{\text{RR},i}^{(w)} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{w-1} \sum_{j=0}^{w-1} \left(\text{RR}_{i-j} - \overline{\text{RR}}_i^{(w)}\right)^2}
$$

where:

- $$\sigma_{\text{RR},i}^{(w)}$$ = rolling standard deviation of RR intervals at position $$i$$

- $$\overline{\text{RR}}_i^{(w)}$$ = rolling mean at position $$i$$

### 4.3 Rolling Trend Slope

Fitting a linear regression within a rolling window quantifies whether the heart rate is accelerating or decelerating:

$$
\text{RR}_{i-j} = \beta_{0i} + \beta_{1i}(i-j) + \epsilon_{i-j}, \quad j = 0, 1, \dots, w-1
$$

The slope coefficient $$\beta_{1i}$$ represents the local trend direction:

- $$\beta_{1i} < 0$$ indicates accelerating heart rate (decreasing RR intervals)

- $$\beta_{1i} > 0$$ indicates decelerating heart rate (increasing RR intervals)

- $$\beta_{1i} \approx 0$$ indicates stable rhythm

## 7.3.5. Domain-Specific HRV Metrics

### 5.1 SDNN: Standard Deviation of RR Intervals

SDNN is the most common global measure of HRV, computed over a recording period:

$$
\text{SDNN} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \left(\text{RR}_i - \overline{\text{RR}}\right)^2}
$$

where:

- $$N-1$$ = total number of RR intervals

- $$\overline{\text{RR}}$$ = mean RR interval over the recording

- $$\text{SDNN}$$ = standard deviation of all normal-to-normal intervals

SDNN reflects overall variability and is influenced by both short-term and long-term regulatory mechanisms.

### 5.2 RMSSD: Root Mean Square of Successive Differences

RMSSD specifically captures high-frequency variability by examining consecutive beat-to-beat differences:

$$
\text{RMSSD} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-2} \sum_{i=1}^{N-2} (\text{RR}_{i+1} - \text{RR}_i)^2}
$$

where:

- $$N-2$$ = number of successive differences

- $$\text{RR}_{i+1} - \text{RR}_i$$ = difference between consecutive intervals

- $$\text{RMSSD}$$ = root mean square of those differences

RMSSD is highly sensitive to **parasympathetic nervous system** changes. It filters out slow trends to highlight rapid physiological changes that reflect vagal tone.

>[!Tip]
> RMSSD is the preferred metric for assessing short-term HRV because it is less affected by breathing patterns than frequency-domain measures and computationally straightforward.

## 7.3.6. Example: Computing RMSSD

Suppose:

- RR intervals (in milliseconds) for 10 consecutive beats: $$\{800, 820, 790, 810, 850, 830, 780, 800, 815, 805\}$$

- We compute RMSSD over the full sequence

### Step 1: Compute Successive Differences

$$
d_i = \text{RR}_{i+1} - \text{RR}_i, \quad i = 1, 2, \dots, 9
$$

$$
d_1 = 820 - 800 = 20
$$

$$
d_2 = 790 - 820 = -30
$$

$$
d_3 = 810 - 790 = 20
$$

$$
d_4 = 850 - 810 = 40
$$

$$
d_5 = 830 - 850 = -20
$$

$$
d_6 = 780 - 830 = -50
$$

$$
d_7 = 800 - 780 = 20
$$

$$
d_8 = 815 - 800 = 15
$$

$$
d_9 = 805 - 815 = -10
$$

### Step 2: Square the Differences

$$
d_1^2 = 400, \quad d_2^2 = 900, \quad d_3^2 = 400, \quad d_4^2 = 1600
$$

$$
d_5^2 = 400, \quad d_6^2 = 2500, \quad d_7^2 = 400, \quad d_8^2 = 225, \quad d_9^2 = 100
$$

### Step 3: Compute Mean of Squared Differences

$$
\overline{d^2} = \frac{400 + 900 + 400 + 1600 + 400 + 2500 + 400 + 225 + 100}{9}
$$

$$
\overline{d^2} = \frac{6925}{9} = 769.44
$$

### Step 4: Take Square Root

$$
\text{RMSSD} = \sqrt{769.44} = 27.74 \text{ ms}
$$

### Step 5: Clinical Interpretation

An RMSSD of 27.74 ms for this 10-beat window indicates moderate beat-to-beat variability. For context:

| RMSSD Range | Typical Population | Clinical Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| > 40 ms | Healthy young adults | Good parasympathetic tone |
| 20–40 ms | Middle-aged adults | Moderate variability |
| < 20 ms | Older adults or stressed individuals | Reduced parasympathetic activity |

## 7.3.7. Real-World Application: Arrhythmia Detection

### 7.1 Temporal Context Features

In clinical datasets like the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia database, each annotated beat is accompanied by contextual RR information:

$$
\text{Pre-RR}_i = \text{RR interval immediately preceding beat } i
$$

$$
\text{Post-RR}_i = \text{RR interval immediately following beat } i
$$

where:

- $$\text{Pre-RR}_i$$ = temporal context before the event

- $$\text{Post-RR}_i$$ = temporal context after the event

These features provide the **temporal grounding** necessary to classify rhythm abnormalities. A premature ventricular contraction, for example, typically shows:

- shortened pre-RR interval (early beat)

- prolonged post-RR interval (compensatory pause)

### 7.2 Multi-Lead Features

Multiple ECG leads provide different spatial viewpoints of the same electrical event. Features extracted from different leads are concatenated:

$$
\mathbf{X}_i = \left[ \text{Features}_{\text{Lead I}},\ \text{Features}_{\text{Lead II}},\ \dots,\ \text{Features}_{\text{Lead V6}} \right]
$$

where:

- $$\mathbf{X}_i$$ = feature vector for beat $$i$$

- each lead contributes its own RR context, morphology, and variability metrics

This multi-dimensional view improves classification accuracy by capturing spatial variation in cardiac electrical activity.

## 7.3.8. Critical Engineering Caveats

### 8.1 Coarseness vs. Granularity

>[!Warning]
> A daily average RR interval is too coarse to identify serious cardiac issues. Always strive for beat-to-beat (Holter-level) data if possible. Temporal resolution determines diagnostic sensitivity.

Aggregating to daily means discards the beat-to-beat variability that carries autonomic information. The appropriate granularity is:

$$
\text{Sampling Resolution} \geq \text{Beat-to-beat interval}
$$

### 8.2 Segmentation

Physiological data is heavily influenced by activity state. Features calculated during sleep versus exercise will differ systematically:

| Activity State | Typical RR Pattern | HRV Characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| Sleep | Longer, regular intervals | Higher parasympathetic HRV |
| Exercise | Shorter, variable intervals | Sympathetic dominance, lower HRV |
| Recovery | Gradual lengthening | Transient HRV suppression |

Effective feature engineering involves **segmenting** the signal by activity level before computing variability metrics.

### 8.3 Data Quality

ECG signals are highly susceptible to motion artifacts, electrode noise, and baseline wander. A single bad reading can skew RMSSD and SDNN results:

>[!Warning]
> Always include signal cleaning and anomaly detection before computing HRV features. A single artifactual beat can inflate RMSSD by orders of magnitude.

Preprocessing steps should include:

- R-peak detection validation

- Outlier interval removal (e.g., RR < 300 ms or RR > 2000 ms)

- Baseline correction

## 7.3.9. Factors Affecting HRV Feature Quality

### 9.1 Recording Duration

Longer recordings provide more stable HRV estimates:

| Duration | Metric Stability | Clinical Use |
|---|---|---|
| 5 minutes | Moderate | Short-term assessment |
| 24 hours (Holter) | High | Comprehensive autonomic evaluation |
| Multiple days | Very high | Long-term trend analysis |

### 9.2 Age and Physiological State

HRV decreases with age and is modulated by:

- circadian rhythm (lower at night for some measures)

- medication effects (beta-blockers increase HRV)

- disease state (diabetes reduces HRV)

These factors must be controlled or stratified in comparative analyses.

### 9.3 Signal Quality

The signal-to-noise ratio of the raw ECG directly affects R-peak detection accuracy:

$$
\text{SNR} = \frac{\text{Power of R-peak signal}}{\text{Power of noise}}
$$

Low SNR increases false positive and false negative R-peak detections, propagating error into all derived HRV metrics.

## 7.3.10. Common Misinterpretations

### Interpretation 1

>[!Warning]
> "Low heart rate always indicates high HRV."

Wrong. A low heart rate with very regular intervals (e.g., in a highly trained athlete with pathological rigidity) can coexist with low HRV. HRV measures variability, not rate.

### Interpretation 2

>[!Warning]
> "RMSSD and SDNN measure the same thing."

Wrong. SDNN reflects overall variability influenced by both short-term and long-term mechanisms. RMSSD specifically captures short-term, beat-to-beat variation driven primarily by parasympathetic activity. They are complementary, not redundant.

### Interpretation 3

>[!Warning]
> "A single abnormal RMSSD value confirms pathology."

Wrong. HRV is highly variable within individuals across time of day, activity, and emotional state. Clinical diagnosis requires repeated measurements and comparison to population norms, not isolated values.

## 7.3.11. Conclusions

Domain-informed feature engineering transforms raw ECG voltage data into structured metrics that capture autonomic function, cardiac variability, and arrhythmia context.

### 11.1 Anatomy of Medical Feature Engineering

The structure of every cardiac feature set follows:

$$
\text{Feature Vector}_i = \left[ \text{Lags}_i,\ \text{Rolling}_i,\ \text{Global HRV}_i,\ \text{Context}_i \right]
$$

- **Lag features**: Encode beat-to-beat memory and short-term rhythm patterns

- **Rolling features**: Summarize local trend and recent variability

- **Global HRV metrics** (SDNN, RMSSD): Capture overall autonomic nervous system function

- **Context features** (Pre-RR, Post-RR): Provide temporal grounding for abnormality detection

### 11.2 HRV Metric Comparison

| Metric | Time Scale | Primary Signal | Autonomic Component |
|---|---|---|---|
| SDNN | Entire recording | Overall variability | Mixed (sympathetic + parasympathetic) |
| RMSSD | Beat-to-beat | Short-term variation | Primarily parasympathetic |
| Rolling Mean | Local window | Local trend | Context-dependent |
| Rolling Std | Local window | Local volatility | Context-dependent |

### 11.3 Critical Principles

Understanding the physiological and statistical nature of cardiac data is essential:

- **Granularity matters**: Beat-to-beat resolution is necessary for meaningful HRV analysis

- **Context matters**: Pre- and post-event intervals provide diagnostic temporal grounding

- **Quality matters**: Signal artifacts propagate through all derived metrics and must be removed

>[!Tip]
> "The best cardiac models combine signal processing rigor with physiological insight. SDNN reveals overall autonomic health; RMSSD reveals parasympathetic tone; temporal context reveals rhythm pathology."

You are now equipped with the technical foundation to engineer domain-specific features from physiological time series data. The next step is implementing rigorous signal preprocessing and quality control to ensure your HRV metrics reflect true cardiac physiology rather than artifactual noise.
