# 7.1.3. Core Characteristics of Time Series Data

### 3.1 Temporal Dependency (Autocorrelation)

Future values depend on past observations. Formally, the autocorrelation at lag $$k$$ is:

$$
\rho_k = \frac{\text{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t-k})}{\sigma_{Y_t} \sigma_{Y_{t-k}}}
$$

where:

- $$\rho_k$$= autocorrelation coefficient at lag $$k$$

- $$Y_t$$= value at time $$t$$

- $$Y_{t-k}$$= value at time $$t-k$$

- $$\sigma_{Y_t}$$ = standard deviation of the series

Positive autocorrelation means high values tend to follow high values; negative autocorrelation means high values tend to follow low values.

### 3.2 Trend and Seasonality

**Trend** is a long-term directional movement in the series. Mathematically:

$$
T_t = f(t)
$$

where $$f(t)$$ is a slowly changing function of time (often linear or polynomial).

**Seasonality** is a recurring pattern at fixed intervals. For monthly data with annual seasonality:

$$
S_t = S_{t+12}
$$

where the seasonal component repeats every 12 periods.

### 3.3 Stationarity vs. Non-Stationarity

A stationary time series has statistical properties that do not change over time:

$$
E(Y_t) = \mu \quad \text{(constant mean)}
$$

$$
\text{Var}(Y_t) = \sigma^2 \quad \text{(constant variance)}
$$

$$
\text{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t-k}) = \gamma_k \quad \text{(covariance depends only on lag } k\text{)}
$$

Non-stationary series violate one or more of these conditions. Most forecasting models assume stationarity; non-stationary data must be transformed (differencing, detrending) before modeling.

### 3.4 Temporal Order

The requirement to maintain sequence is absolute. Shuffling time-ordered data destroys the underlying dependency structure and produces misleading results.

>[!Warning]
> Never shuffle time series data before splitting into train and test sets. Temporal order must be preserved to prevent data leakage from the future into the past.
